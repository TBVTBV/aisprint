## 1. The Problem & Background

A large share of every pediatric pulmonology appointment is spent not on examination or decision-making, but on *reconstructing the story*. The clinician must assemble a coherent clinical picture — symptom pattern and triggers, inhaler technique and adherence, prior hospitalisations and ED visits, treatment responses, and environmental exposures — from input that arrives partial, out of order, and often supplied by a caregiver rather than the child. Families frequently do not know which details matter, so the most decision-relevant facts surface late, incompletely, or not at all.

This is a widely documented burden, not a local quirk. In a study of roughly 100 million outpatient visits, physicians spent an average of about **16 minutes per encounter working in the electronic health record**, with chart review (~33%) and documentation (~24%) the two largest components — i.e. gathering and recording the history dominates the digital work of a visit (Overhage & McCallie, *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2020).

Pediatric pulmonology compounds this in three specific ways:

- **Proxy-reported history.** The person with the symptoms (the child) is usually not the person answering. Parent-proxy and child self-report routinely diverge, and caregiver recall of episodic events is imprecise — so the raw input is inherently noisy.
- **Under-recognised environmental exposures.** Home and environmental triggers — a central driver in pediatric asthma, the dominant condition in this clinic — are frequently under-reported because families do not recognise them as relevant (as seen in trigger-exposure data from the Childhood Asthma Management Program).
- **Structured tools capture control, not context.** Validated instruments such as the Childhood Asthma Control Test (c-ACT) exist, but they measure current control in a handful of items; they do not capture the broader, longitudinal history the physician still has to reconstruct by hand.

The consequence: clinician time is diverted from reasoning to data-gathering, the history that reaches the point of care is incomplete or unstructured, and families leave uncertain about what to prepare for next time. This project focuses on the moment *before* the visit — where a better-structured, family-friendly intake could change what information exists when the appointment begins.

## 2. Research Focus & Scope

**In scope:** How clinical history and symptom information is collected from families *before* a pediatric pulmonology appointment — the channels used today (paper forms, portals, phone calls, or nothing), what information is requested vs. what is actually needed, why families struggle to know what matters, and how the intake output lands (or fails to land) in the clinician's hands at the start of the visit.

**Out of scope (for this sprint):** real-time in-consultation documentation, post-visit follow-up and care plans, diagnosis or triage decision-making, and any handling of identifiable patient data. These are acknowledged as adjacent and may surface in interviews, but the research effort is deliberately concentrated on pre-visit intake so findings are deep rather than thin.

## 3. Goals & Objectives

**Goal.** Understand how pre-visit history is collected today in pediatric pulmonology, where it breaks down, and what an improved, family-friendly intake would need to do — grounded in evidence from real stakeholders, not assumption.

**Objectives:**

1. **Map the current pre-visit intake journey** across each stakeholder (family, secretary, nurse, physician) — steps, tools, hand-offs, and failure points.
2. **Identify which clinical details matter most** to physicians and how often those details are missing or unstructured at the point of care.
3. **Understand the family's experience** — what they are asked, what confuses them, and what would help them arrive prepared.
4. **Surface constraints** — clinical workflow, regulatory/privacy, language and literacy, and technology realities — that any solution must respect.
5. **Produce evidence that sharpens the Research Report** — patterns, quotes, and opportunity areas that become the springboard into the solution concept.

## 4. Research Questions

**Primary question.** How is clinical history collected from families before a pediatric pulmonology visit, and why does the information that reaches the clinician end up incomplete, unstructured, or hard to access?

Sub-questions, mapped to method and stakeholder:

| Research question | Primary source | Method |
|---|---|---|
| **RQ1** — What information is requested from families before the visit today, and through what channels? | Secretaries, nurses, families | Interviews, artefact review |
| **RQ2** — Which details do physicians most need up front, and which are most often missing or unusable? | Pediatric pulmonologists | Interviews |
| **RQ3** — Where do families get stuck — what do they not understand, forget, or not think to mention? | Families & caregivers | Interviews |
| **RQ4** — What happens to intake information between submission and the start of the consultation? | Secretaries, nurses, physicians | Interviews, journey mapping |
| **RQ5** — What constraints (privacy, workflow, language/literacy, tech) must a solution respect? | All stakeholders + desk research | Interviews, desk research |

**Working assumptions to test (not conclusions):** that intake today is mostly unstructured or absent; that families lack guidance on what matters; and that structured pre-visit capture could reduce reconstruction time. The research exists to confirm, refine, or overturn these — we will actively look for evidence that we are wrong.

## 6. Methodology

A mixed, qualitative approach running on two parallel tracks so we are not blocked waiting on interview scheduling.

### 6.1 Desk research (started now)

- **Existing evidence** — prior studies and clinical guidelines on pediatric asthma history-taking, control instruments (c-ACT), and documentation burden; plus any intake forms or materials the partner hospital can share.
- **Competitive & landscape scan** — current pre-visit intake and patient-reported-data tools (e.g. generic digital intake platforms such as Phreesia; validated symptom/control questionnaires) and where they fall short for pediatric pulmonology specifically.
- **Context & constraints** — regulatory and privacy expectations for minors' health data, clinical workflow realities, and language/literacy considerations for families.

### 6.2 Semi-structured interviews

One-to-one, 20–40 minutes, in person where possible and remote where not. Tailored guides per stakeholder, but a shared spine: current process → a concrete recent example → what breaks → what would help. Following the sprint's interviewing guidance:

- **Do** — get informed consent and explain who we are and why; ask open questions, then stay quiet and listen; follow the emotion ("tell me about a time…"); be friendly so people share real pain points; respect privacy and never record without permission.
- **Don't** — lead the witness or pitch a solution; ask for or store identifiable patient data; rush families in a hard moment; or mistake one vivid story for the whole truth.

### 6.3 Journey mapping & synthesis

We assemble findings into a pre-visit intake journey map per stakeholder, then affinity-cluster observations into themes. Each theme is backed by at least two independent sources before we treat it as a pattern — one story is a lead, not a finding.

## 7. Ethics, Consent & Privacy

A pediatric hospital setting raises the bar. Our standing rules for this research:

- Verbal informed consent at the start of every conversation; participation is voluntary and can stop at any time.
- No collection or storage of identifiable patient data. We speak to clinicians and to caregivers about process and experience — never about a specific identifiable child's record.
- No recording without explicit permission; default to written notes. Notes are de-identified and stored only with the team.
- Extra care with families, who may be interviewed near an anxious clinical moment — we never rush them and give an easy way to decline.
- We work through the partner hospital's contact and defer to any local ethics, access, or data-handling requirements they specify.

## 10. Limitations & Risks

- **Small, convenience sample.** 8–12 interviews in one hospital cannot represent all settings; we report findings as directional, not statistical.
- **Access & timing risk.** Clinician availability in a single working week is the main threat; desk research runs in parallel to de-risk this, and we prioritise the two highest-signal groups first.
- **Proxy perspective.** We mostly hear about the child's history from adults; we treat caregiver accounts as one lens, not ground truth.
- **Researcher bias.** Having a solution in mind can steer questions; we mitigate with non-leading guides and by actively seeking disconfirming evidence.
